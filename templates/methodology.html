Prediction Methodology

How Tri Tracker estimates your race finish times.

{# ── Overview ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Overview

The predictor uses your recent training data to estimate finish times for six race formats: 10k, Half Marathon, Marathon, Olympic Triathlon, Half Ironman, and Full Ironman. For each race it calculates a time per leg (swim, bike, run) separately, then adds fixed transition times to produce a total.

{# ── Training window ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Training window

The model looks at the 90 days of activities immediately before the prediction date. Only non-commute activities are included; activities shorter than 1 km are ignored.

If no activities exist within the 90-day window for a required discipline, the model falls back to the most recent activity ever recorded for that sport. This ensures a prediction is always shown, though the confidence score will be lower.

{# ── Weighted average pace ─────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Weighted average pace

Rather than a simple average, each activity is weighted by its distance. A 20 km run represents your sustainable pace more faithfully than a 3 km jog, so it contributes proportionally more to the average.

weighted_speed = Σ(speed × distance) ÷ Σ(distance)

This produces one reference speed in metres per second for each discipline, along with a weighted average training distance used in the next step.

{# ── Riegel's formula ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Distance adjustment — Riegel’s formula

Your training pace cannot be held forever. Riegel’s endurance formula (empirically derived from race results across all distances) captures the relationship between distance and sustainable speed:

Trace = Ttraining × (Drace / Dtraining)1.06

Rewritten in terms of speed (since speed = distance ÷ time):

speedrace = speedtraining × (Dtraining / Drace)0.06

The exponent 0.06 means that doubling the race distance produces roughly a 4% speed penalty. This is well-validated for running up to marathon distance and is used as a reasonable approximation for cycling and swimming as well.

{# ── Open water penalty ────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Open water swim penalty

Strava swim data comes from pool sessions. Open water racing is slower due to navigation, waves, and the absence of walls to push off. For triathlon swim legs, the model applies a 10% speed reduction to your pool pace before the Riegel adjustment. This is applied before calculating the leg time.

{# ── Race formats ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Race formats and transition times

Transition times (T1 = swim-to-bike, T2 = bike-to-run) are not available in Strava data — Strava records each leg as a separate activity. The model uses fixed estimates representing typical amateur times:

Race Swim Bike Run T1 T2

Half Marathon— — 21.1 km — —

{# ── Confidence score ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Confidence score

The confidence score (0–100%) reflects data quality. It starts at 100% and deductions are applied per discipline:

  • −20% if fewer than 3 activities in the 90-day window
  • −15% if the data is stale (no activities in the window at all)
  • −10% if average training distance is less than 40% of the race segment distance

The score has a minimum of 10%. The colour coding is green ≥70%, amber 40–69%, red below 40%.

{# ── 12-week history ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
12-week history chart

The history chart shows how your predicted finish time for the selected race has changed over the past 12 weeks. Each point on the chart is calculated independently: the model is run with a cutoff date set to that Monday, using only activities recorded before that date.

A downward trend means your predicted time is improving (getting faster). Gaps in the line indicate weeks where there was insufficient data to make a prediction for a required discipline.

{# ── Limitations ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── #}
Limitations
  • Riegel’s formula becomes less accurate at very long distances (Ironman bike and run). Predictions for full Ironman should be treated as rough guides only.
  • The model does not account for race-day conditions such as heat, wind, elevation, or course profile.
  • It does not model fatigue accumulation across legs in a triathlon — each leg is predicted in isolation as if it were a standalone effort.
  • Pool swim pace is the only swim data available from Strava. Athletes who train primarily in open water will have less accurate swim predictions.
  • Transition times are fixed estimates and do not reflect your personal T1/T2 speed.
